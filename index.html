Credential Transparency

What Your Certificate Does & Doesn't Mean

Upon completion, learners receive a certificate of completion for the selected course. This documents completion of training content aligned with the applicable OSHA or NFPA standard. It does not mean OSHA or NFPA has certified the student, endorsed the course, or issued a license. Employers remain responsible for determining whether employees are trained, qualified, and competent for their assigned duties and site conditions.

📄

Data center hazards fall under several OSHA standards, most commonly 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S (electrical safety), 29 CFR 1910.146 (permit-required confined spaces, applied at an awareness level for raised floors), and 29 CFR 1910.305 (wiring and UPS/battery systems). Several courses also incorporate NFPA 70E (arc flash), NFPA 70/75 (electrical/IT equipment protection), and NFPA 2001 (clean agent fire suppression), since data centers are regulated by a mix of OSHA and industry fire/electrical codes.
OSHA's General Duty Clause requires employers to provide a workplace free of recognized hazards, and specific standards like 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S require electrical safety training for anyone who works on or near energized equipment. Most data center operators, colocation providers, and facilities management contracts require documented arc flash, EPO, and electrical safety training for electricians, facilities technicians, and IT staff who access server rooms and electrical rooms.
NFPA 70E is the national consensus standard for electrical safety in the workplace, covering arc flash and shock hazard boundaries, PPE selection, and safe work practices around energized equipment. Data centers run high-density power distribution, UPS systems, and switchgear that can produce severe arc flash incidents, so NFPA 70E awareness is considered essential for electricians and facilities technicians working in or near electrical rooms.
Certificates of completion do not have a fixed OSHA-mandated expiration date, but many employers and facility operators require annual or biennial refresher training as part of their internal safety programs, especially for arc flash and electrical safety topics. Check your employer's or site's specific refresher policy; the courses are short enough to retake as often as your program requires.
