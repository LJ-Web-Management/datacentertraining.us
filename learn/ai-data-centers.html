AI Data Centers: What High-Density Computing Changes for Facility Teams

GPU clusters for AI training and inference pack far more power into each rack than the servers most facilities were built for. Here is what that changes in power, cooling, structure, safety, and daily operations, and what technicians should learn next.

Key takeaways

  • Traditional enterprise racks commonly draw single-digit kilowatts. AI training racks commonly draw tens of kilowatts, and some current GPU systems exceed 100 kW per rack.
  • At those densities air cooling runs out of room. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling with coolant distribution units (CDUs) is becoming the norm, with rear-door heat exchangers and immersion filling other niches.
  • Power systems get bigger feeds, higher distribution voltages, more available fault current, and large, fast load swings from synchronized training jobs.
  • Liquid in the white space brings new work: water chemistry, leak detection, pressure testing, and new commissioning and safety procedures.
  • The technicians in highest demand understand both electrical and mechanical systems, plus the controls that tie them together.

Why AI clusters are different

A traditional enterprise rack holds a mix of general-purpose servers, storage, and network gear, and it commonly draws power in the single-digit kilowatt range. Many existing data halls were designed around that number: raised floors, perimeter cooling units, and aisle containment sized for a few kilowatts per rack.

AI racks are different. They are filled with servers built around graphics processing units (GPUs) or other accelerators, each server holding several high-power chips plus fast network adapters. A single eight-GPU server can draw as much power as an entire rack of traditional servers. A full AI training rack commonly draws tens of kilowatts, and some current rack-scale GPU systems exceed 100 kW per rack.

Three other traits matter to facility teams:

  • Tight coupling. A training job runs across hundreds or thousands of GPUs that exchange data constantly, so racks sit close together to keep network links short. You cannot spread the load across a half-empty hall to make cooling easier.
  • Synchronized load. Those GPUs compute and then communicate in lockstep, so power draw rises and falls together across a whole cluster.
  • Fast refresh. GPU generations turn over quickly, and each has tended to raise power per rack. Infrastructure built today has to anticipate the next generation.

Power impacts: bigger feeds, higher voltages, faster swings

Packing tens or hundreds of kilowatts into a rack changes every link in the chain described in our guide to how data center power works.

Distribution at higher power

Delivering 100 kW to a rack at 208 V three-phase takes roughly 280 amps. Raising the voltage cuts the current for the same power, which shrinks conductors and losses. That is why AI halls favor 415/240 V three-phase distribution in North America, overhead busway with plug-in tap-off boxes instead of individual whips from remote power panels, and, in some designs, 48 V DC busbars inside the rack in the style of Open Compute Project hardware. Some operators are evaluating higher-voltage DC distribution as rack power keeps climbing.

Upstream, bigger halls mean larger utility services, more and larger transformers, and more switchgear. Long lead times for that equipment now shape project schedules as much as construction does.

Fault current and arc flash energy

Larger transformers and short, heavy conductors raise the available fault current at busway and rack-level equipment. Depending on how quickly protective devices clear a fault, that can raise the arc flash incident energy at equipment technicians open and operate every day, such as busway tap-off boxes and distribution panels.

Any change to transformers, busway, or protective device settings means the short-circuit, coordination, and arc flash studies must be updated, usually with the IEEE 1584 calculation method, and new equipment labels posted. Tasks that used to be routine at the rack may now call for arc-rated PPE. Data Center Arc Flash Safety explains how to read those labels and boundaries.

Power swings from synchronized workloads

In a traditional hall, thousands of independent workloads smooth each other out, so total load changes slowly. In a large training cluster, GPUs move between compute-heavy and communication-heavy phases together, and a job that starts, stops, or pauses to save a checkpoint can swing the load by a large share of the hall's capacity in a very short time.

Those swings matter at every level:

  • UPS systems see repeated, rapid load changes, and their batteries may cycle more than they were designed for.
  • Generators must accept large step loads without excessive voltage or frequency dips. That affects engine sizing, how many units run in parallel, and governor and voltage regulator tuning.
  • The utility cares too, because large oscillating loads can affect grid stability. Expect interconnection studies to ask about it.

Mitigations include GPU power capping and smoothing features in firmware and software, energy storage that absorbs the swings, and UPS and generator selection based on step-load performance, not just steady-state rating.

Redundancy choices

Not every AI workload needs the same protection. Training jobs save checkpoints and can restart after an interruption, so some operators build training halls with less redundancy than a traditional 2N enterprise room, while inference that serves live customers usually gets the full treatment. Those are business decisions, but they change what technicians maintain and how outages are planned. The Tier levels guide explains the trade-offs.

Cooling: from moving air to moving liquid

Every watt the IT equipment draws becomes heat, as the cooling guide explains. Air is a poor carrier of heat, so cooling a rack with air means moving enormous volumes of it. As densities climb into the tens of kilowatts, the fans, containment, and airflow needed become impractical, loud, and power-hungry. That is where liquid takes over.

Why liquid wins

For the same temperature rise, a given volume of water carries roughly 3,500 times as much heat as the same volume of air. A small pipe can do the work of a very large air duct.

Rear-door heat exchangers

A rear-door heat exchanger (RDHx) replaces the back door of a rack with a water coil. Hot exhaust air passes through the coil and leaves the rack close to room temperature. Passive doors rely on the server fans; active doors add their own fans for more capacity. Because no plumbing touches the servers, rear doors are a common way to add density to an existing air-cooled hall.

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling

Direct-to-chip (DLC) cooling pumps coolant through cold plates mounted on GPUs, CPUs, and sometimes memory and voltage regulators. Most single-phase systems use treated water or a water and propylene glycol mix; two-phase systems use a dielectric fluid that boils inside the cold plate. Cold plates remove most, but not all, of a server's heat, so the remaining components still need air, and the room still needs a working air system.

Coolant distribution units and the two loops

Liquid-cooled racks almost never connect straight to the building's chilled water. A coolant distribution unit (CDU) sits between them, and ASHRAE's liquid cooling guidance names the two loops it separates:

  • Facility water system (FWS): the building side, served by chillers, cooling towers, or dry coolers. Larger pipes, larger volumes, and less strict chemistry.
  • Technology cooling system (TCS): the secondary loop that runs through rack manifolds and cold plates. Small passages, tight water quality, and controlled temperature and pressure.

A CDU holds a liquid-to-liquid heat exchanger, redundant pumps, filtration, an expansion tank, sensors, and controls that hold the TCS supply temperature and pressure. CDUs come in-rack, in-row, or as larger floor-standing units serving many racks. Where no facility water is available, liquid-to-air CDUs reject heat into the room, which suits small deployments but simply moves the air cooling problem somewhere else.

Cold plates work well with warm coolant, so the facility water serving them can often run much warmer than traditional chilled water. That allows more hours of economizer cooling and less chiller energy, which the energy efficiency guide covers.

Immersion cooling

Immersion cooling submerges entire servers in a dielectric fluid. In single-phase immersion, the fluid stays liquid and is pumped through a heat exchanger. In two-phase immersion, the fluid boils off hot components and condenses on a coil above the tank. Immersion handles very high densities and removes almost all server fan power, but it needs hardware qualified for it, new handling procedures, and fluid management, and some two-phase fluids are fluorinated chemicals facing growing environmental scrutiny. It remains a smaller niche than direct-to-chip.

Living with liquid: water quality and leak detection

Cold plates have narrow internal channels, so TCS water quality is not optional. Particles clog channels, corrosion products foul surfaces, and biological growth blocks flow. A sound program usually includes:

  • Filtration at the CDU, with differential pressure monitoring so you know when filters are loading up.
  • Coolant chemistry matched to the wetted materials, usually with corrosion inhibitors and biocide, following the cold plate and CDU manufacturers' specifications. Mixing incompatible metals, such as copper and aluminum, invites galvanic corrosion.
  • Regular sampling for pH, conductivity, inhibitor and glycol concentration, and bacteria, with results logged and trended.
  • Controlled top-off with the specified pre-mixed coolant, never whatever water is handy.

Leak detection is layered. Rope or cable sensors run under CDUs, manifolds, and piping; spot sensors sit in drip trays; and flow, pressure, and reservoir level monitoring can reveal a slow loss before anything is visible. Dripless quick-disconnect couplings let technicians remove a server without draining the loop, and some systems run the TCS below atmospheric pressure so a breach pulls air in instead of pushing liquid out. All of it should alarm into the BMS or DCIM platform, with a written response for each alarm.

Treat every leak as an electrical hazard

Liquid near energized equipment creates shock and slip hazards. Leak response procedures should say who isolates what, in what order, and when equipment must be de-energized, before anyone reaches under a rack with a towel. Coolants and dielectric fluids come with Safety Data Sheets, so they belong in your hazard communication program under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1200.

Structural load and the white space

AI racks are heavy. Dense GPU servers, rack power shelves, busbars, manifolds, and the coolant inside them can make a loaded rack several times heavier than a typical enterprise rack. That affects:

  • Floors. Raised floor systems must be rated for the concentrated and rolling loads involved, and many new AI halls use slab floors with power and piping overhead instead.
  • The delivery path. Loading docks, ramps, elevators, and corridors must carry racks that often arrive fully integrated from the factory.
  • Overhead structure. Busway, cable tray, and piping hung from the structure need engineered supports and, in seismic areas, bracing.
  • People. Moving these racks takes the right equipment, a planned route, and enough hands. Tip-overs and manual handling injuries are real risks, which Server Room Ergonomics and Manual Handling addresses.

Liquid cooling also adds supply and return headers, isolation valves, and drain points to the white space. They need to stay accessible for maintenance and, where possible, routed away from electrical equipment.

Commissioning liquid-cooled systems

Liquid cooling adds a layer of commissioning that air-cooled halls never needed. Within the usual commissioning levels described in the commissioning guide, the liquid scope typically runs like this:

  1. Flush and clean

    Piping is flushed with temporary strainers and filtration to remove construction debris before any cold plate is connected. Skipping or rushing this step is a common cause of early clogging.

  2. Pressure and leak test

    Every joint is pressure tested to the specified value and hold time, and manifolds and quick-disconnects are inspected. Results are documented joint by joint.

  3. Fill and verify chemistry

    The loop is filled with the specified coolant, vented of air, and sampled to confirm chemistry before IT equipment is connected.

  4. Functional tests

    CDU pumps fail over, valves move to their fail positions, control loops hold supply temperature and pressure, and every alarm reaches the BMS.

  5. Integrated and failure testing

    Liquid-cooled load banks or the IT load itself provide heat, including step loads that mimic AI swings. Tests fail a CDU pump, interrupt facility water, and drop utility power to prove the system rides through.

Thermal ride-through deserves special attention. A liquid loop holds little stored cooling and GPUs heat quickly, so a pump or power interruption can lead to throttling or shutdown fast. Many designs put CDU pumps on UPS power for that reason. Infrastructure Commissioning & Startup covers test planning and integrated systems testing.

Operations and safety changes

Day-to-day work changes in several ways once liquid and high power reach the rack:

  • Shared ownership. Facilities, IT, and vendors all touch the liquid loop. A clear responsibility matrix says who owns the CDU, the manifolds, the quick-disconnects, and the cold plates, and who responds to each alarm.
  • New procedures. Filter changes, coolant top-off, CDU pump swaps, and manifold isolation each need a written method of procedure (MOP) with a back-out plan.
  • Hazardous energy beyond electricity. Pumps, pressurized lines, and hot fluid must be isolated under lockout/tagout, not just electrical breakers.
  • More to monitor. Supply and return temperatures, flow, differential pressure, and coolant quality join power and air temperature on every shift's watch list. Monitoring, Automation & BMS Systems covers trending and alarm management.
  • Higher energy at the rack. Updated arc flash labels and PPE requirements change how tasks like installing a busway tap-off box are planned.
  • Noise and heat. High-speed server fans in air-cooled sections can be loud enough to warrant a noise assessment under OSHA's hearing conservation rule (29 CFR 1910.95), and hot aisles at high density can be punishing places to work.

The Data Center Safety Guide covers the electrical and confined space basics that still apply, and the operations guide explains change management and MOPs.

Grid constraints and site selection

Power availability now drives where data centers get built. A U.S. Department of Energy report prepared by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that data centers used about 4.4% of total U.S. electricity in 2023 and projected that share to reach about 6.7% to 12% by 2028.

In practice, that growth shows up as:

  • Long waits for power. Utility interconnection studies, new substations, and transmission upgrades can take years in constrained regions.
  • Power-first site selection. Developers look for available power first, then fiber, land, water, climate, and local workforce.
  • More on-site energy. Larger generator plants, battery energy storage, and growing interest in other on-site sources.
  • Water trade-offs. Evaporative cooling saves energy but consumes water; closed-loop dry coolers save water but use more energy in hot weather. Warm-water liquid cooling helps by widening the hours dry coolers can carry the load. Track water usage effectiveness (WUE) alongside PUE.

Existing sites feel it too, as owners try to squeeze more IT capacity out of the power they already have. That makes Capacity Planning & Forecasting a core skill rather than a once-a-year exercise.

The skills gap and what technicians should learn

Staffing was tight before AI arrived. In the Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2025, nearly two-thirds of operators reported difficulty retaining staff, finding qualified candidates, or both. High-density facilities raise the bar further, because the work crosses traditional trade lines: an electrician needs to understand the cooling loop, and an HVAC technician needs to respect the busway overhead.

If you work in data centers, or want to, these are the skills worth building:

  • Liquid cooling fundamentals: pumps, valves, heat exchangers, pressure drop, and how CDUs are controlled. See Mechanical Systems & Equipment Maintenance.
  • Water treatment basics: what pH, conductivity, and inhibitor readings mean, and when to escalate.
  • Electrical at higher power: three-phase distribution, busway, fault current, and safe work practices aligned with NFPA 70E. Start with Power Systems & Electrical Fundamentals.
  • Controls and monitoring: BMS points, alarms, trends, and how to spot a developing problem in the data.
  • Commissioning and procedures: reading test scripts and writing and following MOPs.
  • Capacity and efficiency math: kW, tons, PUE, and how density changes them. Energy Efficiency & PUE Optimization covers the metrics.

The roles that feel this most are HVAC technicians, critical facilities technicians, controls technicians, and electricians. Licensed work still requires the state license, and opening refrigerant circuits on chillers or DX units requires EPA Section 608 certification from an EPA-approved program. Frequently asked questions

What rack density counts as high density?

There is no single official cutoff. Traditional enterprise racks commonly draw single-digit kilowatts, and many operators start calling a rack high density once it reaches the low tens of kilowatts, where conventional air cooling starts to struggle. AI training racks commonly run at tens of kilowatts, and some current GPU systems exceed 100 kW per rack.

Does liquid cooling eliminate the need for air cooling?

Usually not. Direct-to-chip cold plates remove most of the heat from GPUs and CPUs, but power supplies, memory, storage, and network gear still shed heat into the air, and the room has to stay workable for people. Most liquid-cooled halls keep a smaller air system alongside the liquid loop. Immersion cooling comes closest to removing air cooling at the rack.

Is it safe to run water near servers?

It can be, when the system is designed and operated for it. Liquid-cooled racks use a separate, treated technology cooling loop, dripless quick-disconnects, layered leak detection, and alarms tied to the BMS. Good commissioning, water quality management, and written leak response procedures are what keep the risk low.

What should a facility technician learn first to work in AI data centers?

Start with the fundamentals you will use every shift: how power is distributed and how to work safely around it, how heat moves through air and liquid cooling systems, and how the BMS reports what is happening. Then add liquid cooling specifics such as CDU operation, coolant chemistry, and leak response, along with commissioning and written procedures.
