## What changes from state to state

- **The enforcing agency.** In State Plan states, inspections and citations come from the state agency, which may have its own standards. California's Title 8 Electrical Safety Orders are the best-known example.
- **The electrical code edition.** States and cities adopt the National Electrical Code on their own schedules, sometimes with amendments, which affects installation rules for UPS, battery and emergency systems.
- **The fire code.** Clean agent, battery room and impairment rules follow the fire code your jurisdiction adopts and the local fire marshal's interpretation.
- **Electrician licensing.** Installation work needs the license your state or city requires. Training certificates do not replace a license.
- **Environmental permits.** Generator run-hour limits and fuel storage rules come from state air and environmental agencies as well as the EPA.

What does not change: OSHA's training requirement (or a State Plan's equivalent), the NFPA 70E practices most employers adopt, and the rule that only the employer can designate a qualified person. ## Notes by region

### Northern Virginia and Maryland

The largest data center market in the world runs under Virginia's own State Plan, and Maryland has its own plan too. Multi-site teams that cross the Potomac should confirm both states' rules and keep one set of training records that satisfies the stricter one.

### Atlanta and the Southeast

Georgia and Florida employers fall under federal OSHA. North Carolina, South Carolina and Tennessee run State Plans. Fast hiring in the Atlanta market means many new technicians arrive with no electrical safety background, so awareness training on day one matters.

### Texas

Federal OSHA covers private employers across Dallas-Fort Worth, San Antonio, Austin, Houston and the new West Texas campuses. Large new builds mean many workers move between construction (29 CFR 1926) and operations (29 CFR 1910) rules within months.

### Arizona, Utah, Nevada and the West Coast

Phoenix, Salt Lake City, Reno, Las Vegas, Oregon, Washington and California all fall under State Plans. Heat illness programs are a priority in the desert markets, alongside electrical safety, and California adds its own Electrical Safety Orders.

### Ohio, Illinois and the Midwest

Federal OSHA covers private employers in Ohio and Illinois. Iowa, Indiana, Minnesota and Michigan run State Plans. Central Ohio and Chicago both have a mix of colocation and hyperscale sites, so training needs range from awareness for IT staff to switching for critical facilities technicians.

## Hands-on training near you

Online courses cover the knowledge. The hands-on part has to happen on real equipment, and these are the usual sources in any market:

1. Your employer's electrical safety program and senior electricians, who observe and sign off each person.
2. Equipment manufacturers and their service partners, for specific switchgear, UPS and generator models.
3. Union apprenticeship programs and community college electrical programs, many of which now run data center tracks.
4. Local electrical training centers, safety consultants and NFPA's instructor-led classes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where can I get data center electrical safety training in Northern Virginia?

Our data center electrical safety, NFPA 70E-based and arc flash courses are online, so technicians in Ashburn, Loudoun County and Prince William County can take them on any shift. Virginia runs its own OSHA-approved State Plan (VOSH), so confirm any Virginia-specific rules with your safety team, and get hands-on qualification from your employer.

### Is there data center electrical safety training in Atlanta?

Yes. Our courses are online and available in Atlanta, Marietta, Norcross and the rest of Georgia. Federal OSHA covers private-sector employers in Georgia. Pair online training with site-specific, hands-on instruction from your employer or a local electrical training center.

### Does online NFPA 70E training count in states with their own OSHA plan?

State Plans must be at least as effective as federal OSHA, and federal OSHA allows classroom or on-the-job training. Online courses can deliver the classroom portion in any state. Some states, such as California with its Electrical Safety Orders, add their own rules, so check them with your safety professional. In every state the employer decides who is qualified.

### Is there arc flash or NFPA 70E training near me?

Our arc flash and NFPA 70E-based courses are online and available anywhere in the United States, including Texas, Arizona, Ohio, Illinois, California, Utah, Maryland and Montana. For the hands-on part, ask your employer, local electrical contractors and training centers, union apprenticeship programs, or NFPA's instructor-led classes.

### Which OSHA agency covers data centers in my state?

Federal OSHA covers private-sector employers in most states. About two dozen states and territories run OSHA-approved State Plans that cover private employers too, including Virginia, Maryland, Arizona, California, Utah, Nevada, Oregon, Washington, Iowa, Indiana, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Kentucky, Minnesota, Michigan, New Mexico and Wyoming.
