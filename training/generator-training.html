Generator Training for Data Center Teams

Standby generators run for a few hours a month and then, on the worst day of the year, carry the whole facility. Here is what generator training should cover for operators and technicians, how testing and fuel management fit in, the safety basics, and the courses that build the knowledge.

Short answer: Generator training for data center teams covers how standby generators and transfer switches work, how they are tested under load, how fuel is managed, and how to work safely around running engines. Facility operators need enough training to inspect, monitor, and respond; generator technicians need deeper, hands-on, equipment-specific training to service engines, alternators, and controls.

Key takeaways

  • Operators and technicians need different training. Operators run rounds, support tests, and respond to outages; technicians diagnose and repair the machine.
  • Good training covers generator theory, transfer schemes, load testing, fuel systems, and the NFPA 110 approach to inspection and testing.
  • A generator in automatic mode can start without warning. Lockout, noise, exhaust, fuel, and backfeed hazards belong in every program.
  • Online courses build the knowledge. Hands-on, equipment-specific training and employer authorization are still required for service work.

Generator technician training vs. facility operator training

Facility operators need to know what a healthy generator looks like and what to do when it is not; generator technicians need to know why it is not and how to fix it. Most data center facilities staff are operators, while deep engine and controls repair is usually done by dealer or manufacturer technicians.

Generator technician training paths

If you want to become a generator technician, the usual route is a diesel engine or electrical background, followed by dealer or manufacturer training on specific product lines. The Electrical Generating Systems Association (EGSA) runs schools on on-site power generation and offers a Generator Systems Technician Certification with Apprentice and Journeyman levels; check EGSA for current eligibility and exam details. Our online courses add the data center context: how standby power is sequenced, tested, and documented in a mission-critical facility.

What should generator training cover?

Complete generator training covers five topics: generator theory, transfer schemes, testing and load banks, fuel systems, and inspection and maintenance programs.

Generator theory

Know how the diesel or gas engine, alternator, governor, voltage regulator, starting batteries, coolant heaters, and controller work together to start, reach stable voltage and frequency, and accept load within seconds. Common failure points include weak starting batteries, failed coolant heaters, degraded fuel, and control faults. Generator Fundamentals covers engine and alternator basics, fuel systems, and how to read control panel alarms.

Transfer schemes

Paralleling switchgearSeveral generators synchronize onto a common bus, with load added in priority steps.Know the load-add sequence, load shedding, and what happens if one unit fails to close.

Emergency/Standby Power Systems Fundamentals covers ATS operation, sequencing and coordination, and the failure points most likely to prevent a successful transfer. The data center power guide shows where generators and the UPS fit in the full chain.

Testing and load banks

A generator that only runs unloaded is not proven. Monthly exercise under load shows the unit starts and carries real load, and a load-bank test adds an artificial resistive load when the building cannot provide enough. Running diesels at light load for long periods can cause wet stacking, a buildup of unburned fuel in the exhaust, which loaded runs help burn off. Integrated tests that open the utility breaker on purpose prove the whole sequence, from UPS ride-through to retransfer.

Fuel systems

Runtime depends on fuel. Training should cover bulk and day tanks, transfer pumps, fuel polishing, and sampling for water and microbial growth, because stored diesel degrades over time. It should also cover resupply contracts and how fuel levels are logged. Generator Operations & Maintenance covers sizing, load-bank procedures, fuel management, and cold-start protocols.

NFPA 110 maintenance concepts, in general terms

NFPA 110, the Standard for Emergency and Standby Power Systems, covers the performance of the whole emergency power supply system, from the generator through the transfer switches, including installation, maintenance, operation, and testing. It is not a design manual, and it does not decide where a system is required; other codes and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) do that.

  • Level describes how critical the system is. Level 1 systems are installed where failure could result in loss of human life; Level 2 systems are less critical to life safety.
  • Type is the maximum time in seconds the load may be without power. A Type 10 system must restore power within 10 seconds.
  • Class is how many hours the system is designed to run at rated load without refueling.
  • Inspection and testing follow a routine of regular inspections, monthly exercise under load, supplemental load-bank testing when monthly runs cannot reach the required load, and periodic longer-duration tests. For diesel units, the monthly load is commonly specified as a minimum percentage of the nameplate rating or the manufacturer's minimum exhaust temperature.

Generators that serve only IT load may not be covered the same way as systems that serve life-safety loads, but many data centers model their programs on NFPA 110 anyway. Which edition and which requirements apply to your site is a question for your engineer of record and AHJ. Generators are also regulated as stationary engines under federal and state air rules, which limit non-emergency operating hours, so testing schedules must fit the facility's air permit.

Generator safety: what training must cover

Generators combine electrical, mechanical, thermal, chemical, and noise hazards in one machine, and a unit in automatic mode can start at any moment without warning.

  • Unexpected start: before service, the engine must be locked out under the site's lockout/tagout procedure, including controls and starting circuits.
  • Electrical: generator output, paralleling gear, and transfer switches are energized sources. Backfeed and arc flash at switchgear are real risks.
  • Exhaust and heat: carbon monoxide in generator rooms and enclosures, and hot exhaust and engine surfaces.
  • Noise: running generators require hearing protection under the site's hearing conservation program.
  • Fuel and batteries: diesel spills and fire risk, and starting batteries that can release hydrogen and contain corrosive electrolyte.
Knowledge is not authorization

Online generator courses build knowledge and safety awareness. They do not authorize anyone to service generators, operate transfer equipment, or perform lockout/tagout. Your employer decides who may do that work, after equipment-specific training under the manufacturer's procedures.

How often should generator training be refreshed?

There is no single federal interval for generator training, so follow your employer's program. Electrical safety training under NFPA 70E is retrained at intervals not exceeding three years, and OSHA's lockout/tagout rule requires retraining when job assignments, equipment, or procedures change. As a practice, many sites also walk through emergency start and manual transfer procedures every year, review the MOP before every load-bank or integrated test, and retrain after a new generator, controller, or switchgear upgrade.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between generator technician training and operator training?

Operator training prepares facility staff to inspect generators, watch alarms, support tests, and respond to outages using site procedures. Generator technician training goes deeper into the engine, alternator, fuel system, and controls so the technician can diagnose and repair the equipment, and it relies heavily on hands-on, manufacturer-specific instruction.

Are there generator training courses online?

Yes. Online generator training courses work well for theory, transfer schemes, testing concepts, fuel management, and safety. Hands-on skills, such as servicing an engine or making load-bank connections, still need to be learned on real equipment under the manufacturer's procedures and your employer's supervision.

How do I become a generator technician?

Most generator technicians come from diesel engine, electrical, or military power generation backgrounds and learn specific equipment through dealer or manufacturer training. Industry groups such as the Electrical Generating Systems Association offer technician schools and a technician certification program. Online courses help with theory and with how data centers use standby power.

How often should data center generators be tested?

Most programs exercise generators under load every month and add periodic load-bank and longer-duration tests, following NFPA 110 where it applies, the manufacturer's recommendations, and the facility's air permit. The exact schedule depends on what the generator serves, the code edition your jurisdiction adopts, and the authority having jurisdiction.

Does generator training qualify me to service generators?

No. Training builds knowledge. Your employer decides who is authorized to service generators, transfer switches, and fuel systems, based on equipment-specific training and demonstrated skill, and much of that work is done by manufacturer-trained service technicians.
