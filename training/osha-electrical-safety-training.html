OSHA Electrical Safety Training: What OSHA Requires and How to Meet It

Which OSHA electrical standards apply in a data center, who needs training and how much, whether an "OSHA electrical certification" exists, how often to retrain, and what an online course can cover.

Short answer: OSHA requires electrical safety training for every employee who faces a risk of electric shock that the installation does not reduce to a safe level (29 CFR 1910.332). The training must cover the safety-related work practices for that person's job, and qualified persons need more. OSHA does not issue an electrical certification: providers issue certificates of completion, and the employer decides who is qualified.

Key takeaways

  • OSHA's electrical training rule is 1910.332, inside the safety-related work practice standards (1910.331 to 1910.335).
  • Training may be classroom or on-the-job, and its depth must match the risk the employee faces.
  • Lockout/tagout (1910.147) has its own training and certification-of-training requirements.
  • Construction work on a new data center falls under 29 CFR 1926 Subpart K, not 1910.

What does OSHA require for electrical safety training?

The core rule is 29 CFR 1910.332. It applies to employees who face a risk of electric shock that is not reduced to a safe level by the electrical installation, and it names occupations at higher risk, including electricians, electrical and electronic technicians, and industrial machine operators. In a data center that covers facility electricians and critical facilities technicians, and often IT technicians who work inside energized racks and near remote power panels.

  • Content: employees must be trained in, and familiar with, the safety-related work practices in 1910.331 to 1910.335 that pertain to their jobs.
  • Format: classroom or on-the-job training, or both.
  • Depth: the degree of training is determined by the risk to the employee.
  • Qualified persons must also be trained to distinguish exposed live parts from other parts, determine the nominal voltage of exposed live parts, and keep the clearance distances in 1910.333(c).
  • Unqualified persons must be trained in the practices that protect them, such as staying clear of exposed energized parts and knowing when to stop and get a qualified person.

Which OSHA electrical standards apply in a data center?

It depends on whether the work is in an operating facility or a construction project, and on the equipment involved.

Some states run their own OSHA-approved State Plans, which must be at least as effective as federal OSHA and can add rules. See data center safety training by location for which agency covers each major market.

Qualified vs unqualified: who needs which training?

OSHA defines a qualified person as one who has received training in, and has demonstrated skills and knowledge in, the construction and operation of electric equipment and installations and the hazards involved. The designation is task-specific: a technician can be qualified to reset a breaker on one panel and unqualified for medium-voltage switching.

  • Unqualified data center staff (IT technicians, cabling crews, security, cleaning and escorted contractors) need awareness training: what not to touch, safe distances, lockout/tagout rules and how to escalate. The Data Center Electrical Safety course is written for them.
  • Qualified electrical workers need the full work practice content, PPE selection, test-before-touch, and documented skill demonstrations on site. Start with Electrical Safety & Best Practices and Data Center Arc Flash Safety.

The NFPA 70E training page has a side-by-side table of approach limits and training depth for each group.

Is there an OSHA electrical certification?

No. OSHA does not certify electricians, approve electrical safety courses, or issue an electrical safety card. When a job posting asks for "OSHA electrical certification," it usually means one of these:

How OSHA and NFPA 70E fit together

OSHA sets the legal requirement; NFPA 70E explains in detail how to meet it. OSHA has said in letters of interpretation that it does not enforce NFPA 70E directly but may use it to support citations under its own rules, and that following NFPA 70E is an acceptable way to control electrical hazards. That is why most data center electrical safety programs, and most training, are built on 70E. Read the NFPA 70E training and certification guide for what 70E adds.

How often is OSHA electrical safety training required?

The general industry electrical rules do not set a fixed calendar interval. Training must stay current with the work, which in practice means retraining when:

  • A worker's duties change, or they must use practices they do not normally use.
  • New equipment, new technology or changed procedures arrive, such as a lithium-ion UPS or a higher-voltage distribution system.
  • Supervision or an inspection shows a worker is not following safe practices.

Two other clocks usually apply. NFPA 70E requires retraining at intervals not exceeding three years, and OSHA's lockout/tagout rule requires retraining whenever job assignments, machines, equipment, processes or energy control procedures change, plus periodic inspections of each procedure at least annually.

Common OSHA electrical training gaps in data centers

  1. IT staff treated as non-electrical. Technicians who work in live racks next to remote power panels face real exposure and need documented awareness training.
  2. Contractors with no site briefing. Vendors and cabling crews need site rules and escort before they enter electrical rooms.
  3. Energized work by habit. Data center uptime pressure leads to live work that a planned shutdown or concurrent-maintenance path could avoid. Training should cover when energized work is actually justified.
  4. Records that cannot be produced. Keep certificates, course outlines, site-specific sign-offs and LOTO training certification together for each worker.
  5. No retraining after change. New UPS technology, busway retrofits and 800 VDC AI racks all justify targeted retraining. See the 800 VDC safety guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is OSHA electrical safety training required?

Yes, for employees who face a risk of electric shock that is not reduced to a safe level by the installation. 29 CFR 1910.332 requires training in the safety-related work practices that apply to their jobs. Qualified persons need additional training in identifying live parts, determining voltage and keeping the required clearance distances.

Is there an OSHA electrical certification?

No. OSHA does not certify electricians or approve electrical safety courses. Training providers issue certificates of completion, states and cities license electricians, and the employer decides who is qualified. OSHA's lockout/tagout rule does require the employer to certify that training was done, with each employee's name and training dates.

Can OSHA electrical safety training be done online?

The knowledge portion can. OSHA allows classroom or on-the-job training. Online courses cover hazards, work practices, approach distances and lockout/tagout well, but qualified persons also need hands-on instruction and must demonstrate their skills on the equipment they will work on.

Does an OSHA 10 or OSHA 30 card cover electrical safety?

OSHA 10-hour and 30-hour Outreach courses include electrical hazards as one topic, at an awareness level. They are useful, and data center construction sites often require them, but they do not replace the job-specific electrical safety training 1910.332 requires or make anyone a qualified person.

How often is OSHA electrical safety training required?

The general industry electrical rules do not set a fixed interval. Retrain when duties, equipment or procedures change or when a worker is not following safe practices. Many employers follow NFPA 70E, which requires retraining at intervals not exceeding three years, and lockout/tagout retraining is required whenever job assignments, equipment or procedures change.

Which OSHA standards apply to data center electrical work?

In operating facilities: 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S, including the design safety standards (1910.302 to 1910.308) and the safety-related work practices (1910.331 to 1910.335), plus lockout/tagout (1910.147) and electrical protective equipment (1910.137). Construction and major renovation work falls under 29 CFR 1926 Subpart K. Some utility-type medium-voltage and generation work can fall under 1910.269.
