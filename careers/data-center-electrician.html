Data Center Electrician: Jobs, Pay, Training & How to Start

Data centers are among the largest electrical projects being built in the country, and every one of them needs electricians long after the ribbon cutting. Here is what the work involves, what it pays according to federal data, and the apprenticeship and license paths that lead there.

Short answer: A data center electrician installs, maintains, and tests the power systems that keep servers running: switchgear, busway, UPS systems, generators, and the branch circuits that feed each rack. Most come up through a 4- or 5-year electrical apprenticeship and a state license, then work either for contractors building new data centers or on operating sites as critical power technicians.

Key takeaways

  • There are two kinds of data center electrician jobs: construction work for contractors on new builds, and operations work maintaining live sites where an outage costs customers money.
  • The BLS has no "data center electrician" category. Its electricians occupation had a median annual wage of $63,190 in May 2025, and data center pay depends on region, overtime, and the employer.
  • The standard route is a registered apprenticeship (union or merit shop) plus a state or local license. Mission-critical skills come on top of that.
  • Safety rules are stricter than on most commercial jobs. Expect written procedures for every switching step, and energized work only when it is justified and permitted.

What does a data center electrician do?

A data center electrician keeps power flowing from the utility to the servers, with no interruption. On a construction project that means installing and terminating the equipment. On an operating site it means maintaining, testing, and changing that equipment while the IT load stays up.

The trade skills are the ones any commercial or industrial electrician uses, from terminating conductors to following the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70). The difference is scale and consequence: a large campus has dozens of generators and miles of busway, and one wrong breaker operation can drop thousands of servers. The job rewards precision and documentation as much as speed.

What kind of electricians work on data centers?

Two groups do: construction electricians who build new capacity, and operations electricians (often titled critical power technicians or critical facilities technicians) who keep finished buildings running. Many people do both over a career.

What equipment does a data center electrician work on?

Everything in the power path from the utility service to the rack. The data center power guide walks through that chain; here is what electricians actually touch.

  • Switchgear and switchboards. Medium- and low-voltage gear with draw-out breakers and protective relays, set during construction and switched under a written procedure during maintenance.
  • Busway. Overhead busway with plug-in tap-off boxes above the racks. Joint torque and phase orientation matter, and adding a tap-off on live busway is controlled work.
  • UPS systems and batteries. Electricians install input, output, and bypass feeders, battery cabinets, and DC cabling, and support the manufacturer during startup and maintenance. Our UPS Systems Fundamentals course explains bypass modes and why switching sequence matters.
  • Generators and transfer equipment. Wiring generators, paralleling gear, and automatic transfer switches, then supporting load bank tests. See Generator Fundamentals.
  • Terminations. Lugs torqued to the manufacturer's value and marked, plus medium-voltage terminations by qualified splicers. Loose connections cause many of the hot spots infrared scans find.
  • Testing. Insulation resistance, continuity and phasing checks, infrared thermography, and breaker and relay testing. Third-party test firms often follow the ANSI/NETA acceptance and maintenance testing specifications, and their technicians may hold certification offered by the InterNational Electrical Testing Association (NETA).
  • Branch circuits and rack power. Whips, receptacles, and panel schedules for new cabinets, where every circuit must be labeled and its A or B source verified. Power Distribution Systems Fundamentals covers PDUs, RPPs, and redundancy.
Energized-work rules

OSHA's electrical safety-related work practice rules (29 CFR 1910 Subpart S) and NFPA 70E make an electrically safe work condition the default: open, lock out, test for absence of voltage, then work. Energized work is allowed only when de-energizing would create a greater hazard or is infeasible, and it then requires a risk assessment, the right arc-rated PPE, and usually an energized electrical work permit. Many data center operators set even tighter internal rules. Our Data Center Arc Flash Safety Training explains boundaries, labels, and PPE categories.

Are electricians in demand for data centers?

Yes. Federal labor projections, federal energy research, and industry surveys all point the same way: more data center capacity is being built, it needs much more electrical infrastructure, and operators report trouble finding qualified people.

How much do data centers pay electricians?

The BLS does not publish pay for data center electricians as a separate group, so the best federal benchmark for a data center electrician salary is its electricians occupation. The median annual wage for electricians was $63,190 in May 2025; the lowest 10 percent earned less than $42,640 and the highest 10 percent earned more than $108,510 (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook).

What a specific data center electrician earns depends on the local wage scale, union or merit-shop agreements, overtime (BLS notes electricians should expect overtime during scheduled maintenance and on construction sites), shift differentials on operating sites, and travel or per diem on remote projects. Many states and cities now require employers to publish a pay range in job postings, so current postings in your area are the most reliable way to see what a given employer offers. Licensing and data center electrician apprenticeship paths

There is no separate "data center electrician apprenticeship" license. You become an electrician through a standard apprenticeship and license, then gain data center experience. Several sponsors run registered programs:

BLS describes a 4- or 5-year apprenticeship with about 2,000 hours of paid on-the-job training per year plus technical instruction; military or construction electrical experience may shorten it. Licensing is up to your state or locality, and most states require a test on the National Electrical Code and local codes. CareerOneStop's license finder lists requirements by state. How to become a data center electrician

Get into the trade, get licensed, then add mission-critical knowledge and experience. A realistic sequence looks like this:

  1. Meet the entry basics

    A high school diploma or equivalent, solid algebra, a driver's license, and the ability to pass the background checks data center sites require.

  2. Get into a registered apprenticeship

    Apply to a union JATC, an IEC or ABC chapter, or a contractor's registered program. Apply to several, since intake windows and waitlists vary. Ask local contractors whether they are working on data center projects.

  3. Ask for data center project time

    Large data center builds need many apprentices. Time on one teaches you busway, large switchgear, generator and UPS installation, and how commissioning works.

  4. Earn your journey-level license

    Complete your hours and pass your state or local exam. A license opens both construction foreman tracks and operating-site roles.

  5. Learn the mission-critical side

    Study redundancy (N+1, 2N), UPS bypass operation, generator and transfer sequences, methods of procedure (MOPs), and NFPA 70E. Our courses below are built for exactly this gap.

  6. Move into the role you want

    Stay in construction and aim for foreman or commissioning, or apply for critical power or critical facilities technician jobs at operating sites. A clear resume helps; see our data center resume guide.

How hard is it to become a data center electrician?

The hard part is the trade itself: years of apprenticeship and a licensing exam. Once licensed, moving into data center work is mostly about getting onto the right projects and learning the procedures. Operating-site jobs are more selective and want proven experience with energized equipment.

AI data center electrician work

AI data centers are electrically heavier versions of the same building. Racks full of GPU servers draw far more power than typical enterprise racks, so the electrical work shifts toward larger feeders and busway, more transformers and switchgear per building, and rack power delivered at higher amperages. Liquid cooling adds pumps and coolant distribution units that need reliable power of their own.

For an electrician, AI data center electrician jobs use the same skills on bigger projects with tighter schedules, with extra attention to phase balancing, A and B feed verification, and coordination with mechanical trades. Where to find data center electrician jobs

Most electrician data center jobs are posted by electrical contractors, facility services companies, and operators. To find openings near you:

  • Search job boards for "data center electrician," "critical power technician," "critical facilities technician," and "mission critical electrician," plus your city.
  • Ask your local union hall or IEC or ABC chapter which contractors have data center work, since many projects are staffed through them.
  • Check the careers pages of large operators and colocation providers in your region, and filter for facilities or engineering roles.

Frequently asked questions

Do data center electricians need a license?

Usually, yes. Most states require electricians to pass an exam and hold a license, and requirements vary by state and sometimes by city. Apprentices work under licensed electricians while they train. Check the licensing board where you plan to work, and check each job posting, because some operating-site technician roles ask for a license and others ask for equivalent experience.

Is data center electrical work mostly construction?

A large share of current openings are on construction projects, because new campuses and expansions need crews to install switchgear, busway, generators, and branch circuits. Operating sites also hire electricians and critical facilities technicians to maintain those systems for the life of the building, which usually means steadier schedules and shift work instead of project-based work.

Can a commercial electrician move into data center work?

Yes. Commercial and industrial electricians already know feeders, panels, terminations, and the NEC. The main things to add are mission-critical habits: working strictly under written procedures, understanding redundancy so you never drop a live load, and knowing UPS, generator, and transfer equipment well enough to support maintenance on them.

Do I need NFPA 70E training to work as a data center electrician?

Employers are expected to train electrical workers in safe work practices, and NFPA 70E is the consensus standard most U.S. employers use to meet OSHA electrical safety requirements. Expect your employer to provide or require NFPA 70E-based training and to decide whether you are qualified for specific tasks. Online courses build knowledge, but they do not replace employer qualification or hands-on practice.

What is the difference between a data center electrician and a data center electrical engineer?

The electrician installs, maintains, and tests the equipment with their hands and tools. The electrical engineer designs the system, sizes equipment, studies faults and arc flash hazards, and reviews the work, usually with an engineering degree and often a Professional Engineer license. Many experienced electricians move into commissioning and operations roles that sit between the two.
