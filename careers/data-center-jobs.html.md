Key takeaways

- Data centers employ far more than IT staff. Electricians, HVAC and controls technicians, security officers, engineers, and managers keep the facility running.
- Almost every role shares three habits: safety discipline, procedure-driven work, and clear communication across shifts.
- Many people enter from the trades, the military, IT support, or building maintenance, then branch into commissioning, engineering, or management.
- Training teaches you the systems and the vocabulary. Hands-on experience and any licenses a job requires still matter.

## IT and network roles

These roles work with the servers, storage, and network gear that customers actually pay for. They sit closest to the IT equipment and often work alongside facilities staff in the same data hall.

### Data center technician

Data center technicians (also called DCO technicians, data center operations technicians, or remote hands technicians) install, maintain, and remove IT hardware. They rack and stack servers, run and test copper and fiber cabling, replace failed drives and power supplies, and carry out hands-on tasks for customers or engineers who are not on site.

- **A typical day:** working a ticket queue of hardware swaps, cable runs, and new deployments; walking the floor for amber fault lights; updating asset records; and following chain-of-custody rules when decommissioning drives.
- **Common backgrounds:** IT help desk, PC and server repair, network cabling installers, and military communications. Some candidates hold IT credentials offered by CompTIA.
- **Key skills:** careful hands, accurate labeling, fiber and copper handling, reading tickets precisely, safe lifting, and awareness of power and airflow in the rack.
- **How to prepare:** learn cabling with [Fiber Optics & Network Cabling](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/fiber-optics-network-cabling.html), learn how sites track assets in [DCIM Platform Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/dcim-platform-fundamentals.html), and build safe habits with [Server Room Ergonomics and Manual Handling](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/server-room-ergonomics-manual-handling.html).
- **Where it can lead:** senior technician, deployment lead, network engineer, data center operations manager, or a move into critical facilities.

**Full guide:** [Data center technician: job description, requirements, and how to become one →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-technician.html)

### NOC technician

Network operations center (NOC) technicians watch the systems that watch the data center. They monitor network, server, and facility alarms, open and route tickets, dispatch on-site staff, and keep customers and engineers informed during incidents.

- **A typical day:** watching dashboards from monitoring tools, the BMS, and DCIM; acknowledging and triaging alarms against runbooks; escalating to on-call engineers; joining incident bridge calls; and writing a clean shift handoff.
- **Common backgrounds:** help desk, technical support, IT operations, and military communications or watch-standing roles.
- **Key skills:** fast and accurate triage, knowing which alarms are urgent (a UPS on battery is not the same as a filter alarm), calm communication, and precise documentation.
- **How to prepare:** learn what facility alarms mean in [Monitoring, Automation & BMS Systems](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/monitoring-automation-bms-systems.html), then practice structured troubleshooting with [Incident Response & Troubleshooting](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/incident-response-troubleshooting.html).
- **Where it can lead:** NOC lead, incident manager, network or systems engineer, or operations manager.

## Critical facilities trades

Critical facilities staff keep power and cooling flowing to the IT load. They work on equipment that can injure people and drop customers if it is handled carelessly, so this is the most procedure-driven corner of the industry.

### Critical facilities technician

Critical facilities technicians (also called critical environment technicians or facilities engineers) are the generalists of the building. They operate, inspect, and maintain the electrical and mechanical infrastructure: switchgear, UPS systems, batteries, generators, chillers, pumps, and CRAH units. They also respond first to facility alarms.

- **A typical day:** rounds with a log sheet (generator block heaters and fuel levels, UPS status, battery room conditions, chilled water temperatures); preventive maintenance work orders; escorting vendors doing manufacturer service; switching under a method of procedure (MOP); and shift turnover.
- **Common backgrounds:** electricians, HVAC technicians, stationary and building engineers, and military veterans from power generation, shipboard engineering, or equipment maintenance.
- **Key skills:** reading one-line diagrams and mechanical drawings, using a meter and thermal camera, lockout/tagout, following MOPs exactly, and recognizing abnormal readings before they become alarms.
- **How to prepare:** start with [Power Systems & Electrical Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/power-systems-electrical-fundamentals.html), add [Mechanical Systems & Equipment Maintenance](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/mechanical-systems-maintenance.html), and learn how maintenance programs work in [Preventive Maintenance Planning](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/preventive-maintenance-planning.html).
- **Where it can lead:** senior or lead technician, shift supervisor, chief engineer, facility manager, or commissioning.

**Full guide:** [Critical facilities technician: the full role guide →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/critical-facilities-technician.html)

### Electrician

Data center electricians install, maintain, and troubleshoot the electrical distribution system from medium-voltage switchgear down to the branch circuits that feed each rack. On operating sites they support UPS and generator maintenance, perform infrared scans, and add circuits for new customer deployments under strict change control.

- **A typical day:** installing whips and branch circuits for new cabinets, updating panel schedules, establishing an electrically safe work condition before work, verifying absence of voltage, and supporting vendor maintenance on breakers and switchgear.
- **Common backgrounds:** union and merit-shop apprenticeship programs, commercial and industrial electrical contractors, and military electricians. Most states and many cities require an electrician license, issued by the licensing jurisdiction.
- **Key skills:** the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70, including Article 645 for IT equipment rooms), NFPA 70E work practices, reading one-lines, phase balancing, and keeping drawings accurate.
- **How to prepare:** build on your trade with [Data Center Arc Flash Safety Training](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-arc-flash-safety.html), [Power Distribution Systems Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/power-distribution-systems-fundamentals.html), and [UPS Operations & Load Testing](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/ups-operations-load-testing.html).
- **Where it can lead:** lead electrician, electrical supervisor, critical facilities engineer, commissioning technician, or chief engineer.

**Full guide:** [Data center electrician: jobs, training, and how to get in →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-electrician.html)

### HVAC technician

Every watt of IT power becomes heat, and HVAC technicians remove it. They maintain and repair CRAH and CRAC units, chillers, cooling towers, pumps, air handlers, and in-row coolers. On newer high-density sites they may also work on liquid cooling equipment such as coolant distribution units.

- **A typical day:** filter and belt checks on CRAH units, checking chilled water supply and return temperatures, water treatment checks on cooling towers, responding to high-temperature alarms, and leak checks on refrigerant systems.
- **Common backgrounds:** HVAC/R trade schools, pipefitter and HVAC apprenticeships, commercial HVAC service, and military HVAC. Technicians who open refrigerant circuits need EPA Section 608 certification from an EPA-approved program.
- **Key skills:** the refrigeration cycle, chilled water systems, airflow management, the ASHRAE recommended inlet range (18-27 °C / 64.4-80.6 °F), and methodical troubleshooting.
- **How to prepare:** sharpen your diagnostics with [HVAC Systems Troubleshooting Essentials](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/hvac-troubleshooting-essentials.html), learn the bigger picture in [Cooling Systems Design & Optimization](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/cooling-systems-design-optimization.html), and review [Cooling System and Refrigerant Safety](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-cooling-refrigerant-safety.html).
- **Where it can lead:** mechanical lead, critical facilities engineer, energy and efficiency engineer, or chief engineer.

**Full guide:** [Data center HVAC technician: the full role guide →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-hvac-technician.html)

### Controls technician

Controls technicians keep the building management system (BMS), electrical power monitoring system (EPMS), and the sensors and controllers behind them working. They make sure the equipment runs the way the sequence of operation says it should and that alarms reach the right people.

- **A typical day:** troubleshooting a sensor that reads wrong, trending data to find out why a CRAH unit is hunting, changing alarm setpoints under change control, updating graphics, and working with IT on network and security for building systems.
- **Common backgrounds:** HVAC controls contractors, low-voltage and instrumentation technicians, and IT or network technicians who like hardware.
- **Key skills:** reading control drawings and sequences of operation, common protocols such as BACnet and Modbus, basic networking, and disciplined alarm management.
- **How to prepare:** start with [Building Automation Systems (BAS) Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/building-automation-systems-fundamentals.html), then [Monitoring, Automation & BMS Systems](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/monitoring-automation-bms-systems.html).
- **Where it can lead:** controls engineer, BMS or EPMS lead, commissioning, or operational technology (OT) security.

## Operations and management

Managers in a data center own outcomes: uptime, safety, budgets, and customer trust. Most of them started as technicians, and the best ones still understand the equipment.

### Facility manager

The facility manager owns the building's critical infrastructure: how well it performs, how it is maintained, who services it, and what it costs. They decide how much risk the site will accept and make sure every maintenance activity is planned and controlled.

- **A typical day:** reviewing overnight events, approving MOPs and change requests, checking preventive maintenance completion and backlog, meeting vendors, planning capital projects, and briefing customers or leadership.
- **Common backgrounds:** chief engineers and senior critical facilities technicians, military officers and senior enlisted leaders, and facility managers from hospitals or manufacturing plants.
- **Key skills:** maintenance program management, risk assessment, budgeting, vendor contracts, leading people, and enough technical depth to challenge a bad plan.
- **How to prepare:** [Facility Management Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/facility-management-fundamentals.html), [Preventive Maintenance Planning](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/preventive-maintenance-planning.html), and [Data Center Operations Management](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-operations-management.html).
- **Where it can lead:** director of critical facilities, regional facilities director, or vice president of operations.

**Full guide:** [Data center facility and operations manager: the full role guide →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-facility-manager.html)

### Operations manager

Data center operations managers run the day-to-day work across shifts. At colocation sites they often oversee facilities, remote hands, and security together, and they answer to customers when something goes wrong.

- **A typical day:** reviewing shift reports and incidents, running change review meetings, tracking service level agreement (SLA) metrics, solving staffing gaps, handling escalations, and leading post-incident reviews.
- **Common backgrounds:** shift supervisors, NOC leads, IT operations managers, and military operations leaders.
- **Key skills:** incident and change management, scheduling, clear writing, customer communication, and turning lessons learned into better procedures.
- **How to prepare:** [Data Center Operations Management](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-operations-management.html), [Incident Response & Troubleshooting](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/incident-response-troubleshooting.html), and [Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/disaster-recovery-business-continuity.html).
- **Where it can lead:** site director, regional operations director, or head of data center operations.

### Capacity planner

Capacity planners track how much power, cooling, space, and network capacity a site has left, and forecast when it will run out. They approve new deployments only when the redundancy design still holds afterward.

- **A typical day:** pulling load data from DCIM and branch circuit monitoring, reviewing deployment requests, checking that A and B feeds stay within their limits, updating growth forecasts, and flagging stranded or constrained capacity.
- **Common backgrounds:** facilities engineers, experienced technicians who like data, and IT infrastructure analysts.
- **Key skills:** spreadsheets and data analysis, DCIM tools, redundancy concepts such as N+1 and 2N, and basic power and cooling math.
- **How to prepare:** [Capacity Planning & Forecasting](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/capacity-planning-forecasting.html), [DCIM Platform Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/dcim-platform-fundamentals.html), and [Energy Efficiency & PUE Optimization](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/energy-efficiency-pue-optimization.html).
- **Where it can lead:** infrastructure planning manager, design engineering, or site selection and strategy roles.

## Engineering, design, and construction

New capacity has to be designed, built, and proven before it carries a single customer server. These roles do that work, often for engineering firms, contractors, and commissioning agencies rather than the site operator.

### Design engineer

Electrical, mechanical, and controls design engineers lay out new data centers and major upgrades. They choose the redundancy topology, size equipment, draw one-line diagrams and cooling plant schematics, and make sure the design meets code and the owner's standards.

- **A typical day:** load calculations, checking drawings, coordinating with architects and other disciplines, reviewing equipment submittals, answering contractor questions, and visiting sites under construction.
- **Common backgrounds:** electrical or mechanical engineering degrees. Senior roles often require a Professional Engineer (PE) license issued by state licensing boards. Engineers who have worked in operations bring a valuable maintainability view.
- **Key skills:** power system and cooling design, codes and standards (NFPA 70, ANSI/TIA-942, ASHRAE guidelines), Uptime Institute Tier concepts, and clear drawings.
- **How to prepare:** [Data Center Design Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-design-fundamentals.html), [Tier Infrastructure Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/tier-infrastructure-fundamentals.html), and [Cooling Systems Design & Optimization](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/cooling-systems-design-optimization.html).
- **Where it can lead:** lead or senior engineer, engineering manager, design director, or owner's representative.

**Full guide:** [Data center engineer: types of engineering roles and how to qualify →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-engineer.html)

### Commissioning agent

Commissioning agents prove that new systems were installed correctly and perform as designed before the owner accepts them. Commissioning is often described in levels: L1 factory witness testing, L2 site delivery and installation inspection, L3 pre-functional checks and start-up, L4 functional performance testing, and L5 integrated systems testing (IST).

- **A typical day:** writing test scripts, witnessing tests with load banks, pulling utility power on purpose during IST, logging issues, verifying fixes, and assembling turnover documents. Travel is common.
- **Common backgrounds:** critical facilities technicians, electricians, controls technicians, and engineers. Some pursue commissioning credentials offered by industry organizations.
- **Key skills:** reading sequences of operation, test procedures, load bank operation, detailed documentation, and the tact to report problems in someone else's work.
- **How to prepare:** [Infrastructure Commissioning & Startup](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/infrastructure-commissioning-startup.html), [Emergency/Standby Power Systems Fundamentals](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/emergency-standby-power-systems-fundamentals.html), and [Monitoring, Automation & BMS Systems](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/monitoring-automation-bms-systems.html).
- **Where it can lead:** commissioning lead or manager, operations readiness lead, or facility manager for a new site.

### Construction trades

Electricians, pipefitters, sheet metal workers, ironworkers, low-voltage installers, and other trades build data centers. Large campus projects keep crews busy for long stretches, and many tradespeople later move into operations at the sites they helped build.

- **A typical day:** a pre-task safety plan, then installing cable tray, busway, conduit, and wire, setting equipment, piping chilled water, or pulling fiber, all coordinated with other trades and the commissioning team.
- **Common backgrounds:** union and merit-shop apprenticeships and trade schools. Many contractors ask for OSHA 10-hour or 30-hour Outreach cards, which are issued through OSHA-authorized trainers.
- **Key skills:** your trade, blueprint reading, construction safety (fall protection, electrical, and excavation hazards), and quality work that passes inspection the first time.
- **How to prepare:** learn what happens after you finish with [Infrastructure Commissioning & Startup](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/infrastructure-commissioning-startup.html), and learn the operating-site safety rules in [Data Center Electrical Safety Training](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-electrical-safety.html).
- **Where it can lead:** foreman, superintendent, commissioning technician, or a critical facilities technician role on the finished site.

## Security, compliance, and safety

Customers trust a data center with their most sensitive equipment and data. These roles protect that trust, and the people working in the building.

### Security officer

Data center security officers control who gets in and what they do once inside. They staff entrances and the security operations center, verify identities, manage badges and biometrics, escort visitors and contractors, and watch CCTV.

- **A typical day:** checking visitors against approved access lists, issuing temporary badges, monitoring cameras, patrolling, inspecting deliveries, responding to door-forced and door-held alarms, and logging everything.
- **Common backgrounds:** contract security, military police, law enforcement, and corrections. Many states require a security guard license.
- **Key skills:** attention to detail, following post orders exactly, preventing tailgating, de-escalation, and clear incident reports.
- **How to prepare:** [Data Center Physical Security and Access Control Training](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-physical-security-access-control.html) and [Access Control & Physical Security](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/access-control-physical-security.html).
- **Where it can lead:** security supervisor, security operations manager, or compliance roles.

**Full guide:** [Data center security jobs: roles, requirements, and training →](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-security-jobs.html)

### Compliance specialist

Compliance specialists make sure the facility can prove it follows the frameworks its customers care about: SOC 2, ISO/IEC 27001, PCI DSS, and the HIPAA Security Rule's physical safeguards. They manage policies, evidence, and audits.

- **A typical day:** collecting evidence such as access review exports, visitor logs, CCTV retention checks, and maintenance records; updating policies; running access reviews; supporting auditors; and tracking remediation items.
- **Common backgrounds:** security operations, IT audit, quality assurance, risk management, and operations administration.
- **Key skills:** knowing what each framework asks for, organized record keeping, clear writing, and following up until gaps are closed.
- **How to prepare:** [Security & Compliance for Data Centers](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/security-compliance-data-centers.html) and [Facility Environmental Compliance](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/facility-environmental-compliance.html).
- **Where it can lead:** compliance manager, governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) lead, or risk management.

### EHS manager

Environmental, health, and safety (EHS) managers run the programs that keep workers safe and the site within its environmental permits. In a data center that means the electrical safety program, lockout/tagout, confined space, PPE, and hazard communication, plus generator air permits, fuel storage, and refrigerant records.

- **A typical day:** site walks, auditing LOTO and work permits, reviewing contractor safety plans, investigating incidents and near misses, tracking training records, and preparing environmental reports.
- **Common backgrounds:** safety professionals, industrial hygiene, environmental science, and construction safety. Some hold credentials offered by the Board of Certified Safety Professionals.
- **Key skills:** OSHA general industry standards (29 CFR 1910), NFPA 70E, environmental regulations, training design, and influencing people without direct authority.
- **How to prepare:** [Facility Environmental Compliance](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/facility-environmental-compliance.html), [Electrical Safety & Best Practices](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/electrical-safety-best-practices.html), and [Lithium-Ion Battery Thermal Runaway and Emergency Response](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/lithium-ion-thermal-runaway-emergency-response.html).
- **Where it can lead:** regional or corporate EHS director.

## Pay, schedules, and outlook

We do not quote salaries, because pay varies by region, shift, role, employer, and experience. For national pay and outlook data on related occupations, check the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, for example the pages for [electricians](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm) and [HVAC mechanics and installers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/heating-air-conditioning-and-refrigeration-mechanics-and-installers.htm).

Schedules depend on the role. Technicians, NOC staff, and security officers often work rotating 8-hour or 12-hour shifts, including nights, weekends, and holidays. Engineers and managers usually work days with on-call duty. Ask about shift rotation, differentials, and on-call expectations before you accept an offer.

Demand is real. In the [Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2025](https://intelligence.uptimeinstitute.com/resource/uptime-institute-global-data-center-survey-2025), nearly two-thirds of operators reported difficulty retaining staff, finding qualified candidates, or both.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a data center technician and a critical facilities technician?

A data center technician works on the IT side: installing servers, running cables, swapping failed parts, and handling remote hands requests. A critical facilities technician works on the building side: the electrical and mechanical systems such as switchgear, UPS systems, generators, chillers, and CRAH units that keep that IT equipment powered and cooled.

### Which data center job is best for beginners?

It depends on your background. People with IT support experience often start as data center technicians or NOC technicians. People with electrical, mechanical, or military maintenance experience often start as entry-level critical facilities technicians. Security officer roles are another common first step into the industry.

### Do data center jobs require a license?

Some do. Electrical work generally requires an electrician license issued by the state or local jurisdiction. Technicians who open refrigerant circuits need EPA Section 608 certification from an EPA-approved program. Many states license security officers. IT technician roles usually have no license requirement, though employers may ask for industry credentials.

### Can I move between IT and facilities roles?

Yes, and many people do. NOC and data center technicians who learn power and cooling fundamentals can move toward facilities, controls, or capacity planning. Facilities technicians who learn monitoring and networking basics are well placed for controls, DCIM, and operations management roles.
