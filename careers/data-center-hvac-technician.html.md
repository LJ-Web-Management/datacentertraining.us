**Short answer:** A data center HVAC technician keeps the cooling plant running around the clock: CRAH and CRAC units, chillers, cooling towers, pumps, and increasingly liquid cooling equipment. It uses the same trade skills as commercial HVAC, but with redundancy, written procedures, and zero tolerance for lost cooling. Technicians who handle refrigerant need EPA Section 608 certification.

Key takeaways

- Data center HVAC jobs are mostly chilled water and precision cooling work on large, redundant systems that never shut off.
- EPA Section 608 certification is required for work that can release refrigerant. Universal certification covers every equipment type you are likely to meet.
- The BLS benchmark for pay is its HVACR mechanics and installers occupation: a median annual wage of $61,010 in May 2025.
- Commercial HVAC technicians move in by adding chilled water, controls, and mission-critical procedure skills.

## What does a data center HVAC technician do?

A data center HVAC technician operates, maintains, and repairs the systems that move heat from the IT equipment to the outdoors. The goal is simple to state and hard to deliver: keep server inlet air inside the target range, usually the ASHRAE recommended range of 18 to 27 °C (64.4 to 80.6 °F), through equipment failures, maintenance, and summer peaks.

A typical shift mixes planned and reactive work:

- **Rounds:** logging chilled water supply and return temperatures, pump pressures, chiller status, tower basin levels, and data hall temperatures.
- **Preventive maintenance:** filters, belts, and bearings on air handlers; coil cleaning; strainer cleaning; valve exercising; tower water treatment checks.
- **Alarm response:** a CRAH unit losing airflow, a high-temperature alarm on a row, or a leak detection cable reporting water under the floor.
- **Vendor support:** escorting chiller and controls technicians, isolating equipment for them, and verifying it returns to service correctly.
- **Records:** refrigerant use, work orders, and shift turnover notes so the next crew knows the plant's exact state.

## How is data center HVAC different from commercial HVAC?

The equipment overlaps, but the purpose changes everything. Commercial HVAC serves people; mission-critical HVAC serves machines that fail when they overheat.

**Commercial HVAC vs. mission-critical data center HVAC**

|  | Commercial HVAC | Data center HVAC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Load | Varies with weather and occupancy; comfort cooling | Almost all sensible heat from IT, running 24/7 and growing as racks are added |
| Redundancy | Often a single unit per zone | Spare units (N+1 or more) and dual paths so any one unit can fail or be serviced |
| How work is done | Service calls and work orders | Written methods of procedure (MOPs), change control, and maintenance windows |
| What success looks like | Occupant comfort and complaints resolved | Server inlet temperatures held in range and no loss of cooling redundancy |
| Efficiency metric | Energy use per square foot | Power usage effectiveness (PUE) and kW of cooling per kW of IT |

The biggest habit change is thinking about redundancy before touching anything. Before you isolate a CRAH or a chiller, you confirm the remaining units can carry the load, that controls will not stage something unexpected, and that you know how to restore it fast. ## What equipment does a data center HVAC technician work on?

Most large sites use a central chilled water plant feeding air handlers in the data halls, with liquid cooling growing on high-density floors.

**Common data center cooling equipment**

| Equipment | What it does | Typical technician tasks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CRAH units and fan walls | Blow data hall air across chilled water coils; a valve controls capacity | Filters, fan motors and belts, valve actuators, sensor checks |
| CRAC units | Self-contained units with a compressor and refrigerant circuit | Refrigerant charge and leak checks, compressor and condenser service |
| Chillers | Produce chilled water; air-cooled or water-cooled, screw or centrifugal | Logging, oil and refrigerant checks, tube cleaning support, vendor escorts |
| Cooling towers | Reject heat from condenser water by evaporation | Water treatment, basin and fill inspection, fan and gearbox service |
| Pumps, valves, heat exchangers | Move and control water; plate exchangers enable economizer (free) cooling | Seal and bearing checks, strainer cleaning, valve exercising |
| CDUs and liquid cooling | Coolant distribution units feed direct-to-chip cold plates or rear-door heat exchangers | Coolant quality, filters, leak response, pump redundancy checks |

AI and other high-density deployments are pushing more sites toward liquid cooling, which brings plumbing-style skills (fittings, flushing, coolant chemistry) into the data hall. [Cooling Systems Design & Optimization](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/cooling-systems-design-optimization.html) covers air and liquid approaches side by side.

## Do you need EPA Section 608 certification?

Yes, for any work that could release refrigerant. Under the Clean Air Act, EPA requires technicians who maintain, service, repair, or dispose of equipment that could release refrigerants to be certified through an EPA-approved certifying organization.

- **Type I:** small appliances.
- **Type II:** high-pressure and very high-pressure equipment, such as most DX CRAC units and many chillers.
- **Type III:** low-pressure equipment, such as some centrifugal chillers.
- **Universal:** all of the above, and the most flexible choice for a data center with mixed equipment.

EPA states that Section 608 certifications do not expire. Keep a copy of your card, since EPA requires certified technicians to keep proof of certification at their place of business. Newer equipment is also moving to lower-GWP refrigerants, some of them mildly flammable (A2L), which adds handling precautions; our [Cooling System and Refrigerant Safety](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-cooling-refrigerant-safety.html) course covers them. That course is safety awareness training and does not provide EPA certification.

## What skills do data center HVAC jobs require?

Strong fundamentals plus the discipline to work on live systems. The skills that show up most often in hvac data center jobs:

- The refrigeration cycle, superheat and subcooling, and reading chiller and CRAC controllers.
- Chilled water and condenser water systems: delta T, flow, pump curves, and valve authority.
- Airflow management: hot and cold aisle containment, blanking panels, and why bypass air wastes capacity.
- Building management system (BMS) navigation: trending points, acknowledging alarms, and reading sequences of operation. [Monitoring, Automation & BMS Systems](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/monitoring-automation-bms-systems.html) is a good primer.
- Lockout/tagout on pumps, fans, and electrical disconnects, plus awareness of electrical and confined space hazards.
- Clear documentation: rounds logs, refrigerant records, and turnover notes.
- Structured troubleshooting under pressure, covered in [HVAC Systems Troubleshooting Essentials](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/hvac-troubleshooting-essentials.html).

## Data center HVAC technician salary: what BLS data shows

The BLS does not track data center HVAC technicians separately. Its heating, air conditioning, and refrigeration mechanics and installers occupation had a **median annual wage of $61,010 in May 2025**; the lowest 10 percent earned less than $40,050 and the highest 10 percent earned more than $95,210. BLS projects 11 percent employment growth from 2025 to 2035, with about 40,600 openings a year.

## How to move from commercial HVAC into data centers

Most data center HVAC technicians come from commercial, industrial, or military HVAC. The move is about proving you can work on critical systems, not learning a new trade.

1. ### Get EPA 608 Universal if you do not have it

   It is the credential postings most often name, and it covers every equipment type on a mixed site.
2. ### Build chilled water and controls experience

   Large commercial buildings, hospitals, and campuses with central plants are the closest match. Ask for chiller, tower, and BMS work.
3. ### Learn mission-critical procedures

   Study redundancy, methods of procedure, change control, and how a site stays concurrently maintainable. [Mechanical Systems & Equipment Maintenance](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/mechanical-systems-maintenance.html) covers maintenance programs for this kind of plant.
4. ### Target the right employers

   Apply to operators, colocation providers, facility services contractors that staff data centers, and mechanical contractors building or servicing them. Search for "data center HVAC technician," "mechanical technician," and "critical facilities technician."
5. ### Rewrite your resume for critical work

   Lead with chillers, tonnage, central plants, BMS, and any 24/7 facilities. Mention zero-downtime work and documentation. See our [data center resume guide](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-technician-resume.html).

If you want to broaden beyond mechanical work, the [critical facilities technician](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/critical-facilities-technician.html) role combines HVAC, electrical, and controls duties and is a common next step.

## Data center mechanical engineer jobs

Data center mechanical engineers design or manage the cooling systems instead of servicing them by hand. Design engineers at engineering firms size chillers, piping, air handling, and liquid cooling for new builds. Operations or facilities engineers on site own plant performance, efficiency, and root cause analysis. Most hold a mechanical engineering degree; BLS notes a Professional Engineering (PE) license is not required for entry-level mechanical engineers but can be earned later for more independence and leadership. Experienced HVAC technicians sometimes move toward these roles through commissioning or facilities engineering. See [types of data center engineers](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-engineer.html) for how these jobs compare.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do data center HVAC technicians need EPA 608 certification?

Anyone who performs work that could release refrigerant, such as connecting gauges, recovering, or charging, needs EPA Section 608 technician certification from an EPA-approved certifying organization. Data centers often have both high-pressure equipment and low-pressure chillers, so Universal certification, which covers all equipment types, is the most flexible choice. Section 608 certifications do not expire.

### Is data center HVAC harder than commercial HVAC?

The equipment is familiar, but the stakes and the process are different. Cooling runs every hour of the year, redundancy has to be preserved during every task, and work follows written procedures with change control. Technicians who are comfortable with chilled water, controls, and documentation usually adapt well.

### What is the difference between a CRAC and a CRAH unit?

A CRAC (computer room air conditioner) has its own compressor and refrigerant circuit, like a large split or packaged system. A CRAH (computer room air handler) has no compressor: it blows air across a coil fed with chilled water from a central plant and controls cooling with a valve. Large data centers mostly use CRAHs or fan walls on chilled water.

### Do data centers hire entry-level HVAC technicians?

Some do, especially on large campuses and through facility services contractors, but many postings ask for prior commercial or industrial HVAC experience. Holding EPA 608 certification, having chilled water or controls experience, and showing you understand mission-critical procedures all make an entry-level application stronger.

### What does a data center mechanical engineer do?

A data center mechanical engineer designs, analyzes, or manages the cooling systems instead of servicing them by hand. Design engineers size chillers, piping, and air or liquid cooling for new builds. Operations engineers own the performance and reliability of an existing plant. Most have a mechanical engineering degree, and senior design roles often call for a Professional Engineer license.
