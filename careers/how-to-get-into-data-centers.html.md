Key takeaways

- Hiring managers look for safety awareness, respect for procedures, mechanical or technical aptitude, and reliability before they look for data center experience.
- The fastest routes in start from adjacent work: IT support, an electrical or HVAC trade, military service, or facilities maintenance.
- A resume that uses the industry's language (MOP, LOTO, NFPA 70E, BMS, preventive maintenance) tells a reader you already think like a data center technician.
- The first 90 days are about learning the site, its procedures, and its people. Nobody expects you to know everything on day one.

## What entry-level employers actually look for

Ask a chief engineer what they want in a new technician and you rarely hear "ten years of UPS experience." You hear about attitude and habits, because the equipment can be taught and habits are hard to change. Most hiring managers are screening for:

- **A safety mindset.** You stop when something looks wrong, you ask before you touch, and you never treat a lockout as optional.
- **Respect for procedures.** Data centers run on written steps. People who improvise cause outages.
- **Technical aptitude.** Electrical, mechanical, or IT. You can use a meter, read a diagram, or troubleshoot logically.
- **Reliability.** You show up for every shift, including nights and holidays, and you hand off work cleanly.
- **Communication.** You can explain what you saw, what you did, and what you need, clearly and calmly.

Many employers also run background checks, because their customers include banks, hospitals, and government agencies.

## The step-by-step plan

Work through these steps in order. Some will take an afternoon and some will take months, depending on where you are starting.

1. ### Learn how a data center works

   Start with the big picture: where the power comes from, how heat leaves the building, and why every system has a backup. Read [What Is a Data Center?](https://datacentertraining.us/learn/what-is-a-data-center.html), then the [power](https://datacentertraining.us/learn/data-center-power.html) and [cooling](https://datacentertraining.us/learn/data-center-cooling.html) guides in our [Knowledge Center](https://datacentertraining.us/learn/). You should be able to explain what a UPS, a generator, and a CRAH unit do in plain words.
2. ### Pick a lane

   "Data center job" covers IT technicians, electricians, HVAC techs, security officers, and more. Take the [career quiz](https://datacentertraining.us/quizzes/career-quiz.html) and read [Data Center Jobs Explained](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-jobs.html) to choose a target role that matches your background. A focused application beats a generic one.
3. ### Build your safety foundations

   Data centers have serious hazards: arc flash, stored energy in batteries, rotating equipment, refrigerants, noise, and open floor tiles. Learn the basics of lockout/tagout (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147) and NFPA 70E electrical safety before your first interview. [Data Center Electrical Safety Training](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-electrical-safety.html) and [Raised Floor and Confined Space Awareness Training](https://datacentertraining.us/courses/data-center-raised-floor-confined-space.html) are good first courses.
4. ### Get hands-on experience

   Employers want proof you can work with your hands safely. IT help desk work, an electrical apprenticeship, an HVAC program, military service, or building maintenance all count. See [the options below](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/how-to-get-into-data-centers.html#experience).
5. ### Consider third-party credentials

   Some roles require a license or certification from an outside organization, and others simply value one. Know which ones matter for your lane. See [the credentials table](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/how-to-get-into-data-centers.html#credentials).
6. ### Write a resume that speaks the language

   Translate your experience into the terms data center hiring managers search for: MOP, LOTO, NFPA 70E, BMS, preventive maintenance, and change management. See [the keyword list](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/how-to-get-into-data-centers.html#resume).
7. ### Know where to look

   Openings are spread across operators, contractors, vendors, and staffing firms, under many different job titles. See [where to search](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/how-to-get-into-data-centers.html#where-to-look).
8. ### Prepare for interviews

   Expect safety questions, technical basics, and scenario questions like "A UPS alarm sounds at 2 a.m. What do you do?" Practice with our [data center interview questions and answers](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/interview-questions.html).
9. ### Plan your first 90 days

   Once you are hired, the learning speeds up. Know what to focus on in your first three months. See [the 90-day outline](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/how-to-get-into-data-centers.html#first-90-days).

## Ways to get hands-on experience

If you have none of these yet, pick the one closest to your target role. Each builds skills data center employers recognize.

**Common paths into data center work**

| Path | What you learn | Where it leads in a data center |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IT help desk or desktop support | Hardware, troubleshooting, ticketing, and customer communication | Data center technician, NOC technician |
| Electrical apprenticeship | Electrical theory, code, installation, and safe work practices, under a licensed electrician | Electrician, critical facilities technician |
| HVAC/R school or apprenticeship | Refrigeration, chilled water, airflow, and controls | HVAC technician, critical facilities technician |
| Military service | Electrical, mechanical, power generation, communications, or security skills, plus procedures and readiness | Almost any facilities, IT, or security role |
| Facilities maintenance | Preventive maintenance, work orders, and building systems in hospitals, campuses, or plants | Critical facilities technician, building engineer |
| Construction on a data center project | How the infrastructure is built and commissioned | Commissioning technician, operations roles on the finished site |

Career changers with years in one of these fields have a head start. Our [guide for career changers](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/career-changers.html) shows exactly what transfers.

## Third-party credentials worth knowing

These credentials are offered by outside organizations, not by us. Which ones you need depends on your role and your state.

**Credentials and the organizations that offer them**

| Credential | Offered by | Most relevant for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrician license | State or local licensing authorities | Electricians performing electrical work |
| EPA Section 608 certification | EPA-approved certifying programs | Technicians who open refrigerant circuits |
| OSHA 10-hour or 30-hour Outreach card | OSHA Outreach Training Program, through authorized trainers | Construction and general industry workers |
| IT credentials such as A+ and Network+ | CompTIA | Data center and NOC technicians |
| Cabling installer and design credentials | BICSI | Cabling technicians and designers |
| Design and operations accreditations | Uptime Institute | Engineers, managers, and senior operations staff |

Where our courses fit

Our courses award a certificate of completion. That is not a license or a certification, and only your employer can designate you a qualified person for electrical work. What a course does give you is a working understanding of the systems, standards, and safe practices you will use on the job, plus a record of training to show an employer.

## Write a resume that speaks the language

Hiring managers and screening software look for specific terms. If you have done the work, use the words the industry uses. Only list what you have actually done, because an interviewer will ask.

**Resume keywords and what they tell a hiring manager**

| Keyword | What it signals |
| --- | --- |
| MOP / SOP / EOP | You follow written methods of procedure, standard operating procedures, and emergency operating procedures. |
| LOTO | You know how to isolate and verify hazardous energy before work. |
| NFPA 70E | You understand electrical safety, approach boundaries, and arc flash PPE. |
| BMS / BAS / EPMS | You can read building and power monitoring systems and respond to alarms. |
| Preventive maintenance (PM) and CMMS | You complete and document scheduled maintenance in a work order system. |
| Change management | You understand that work on live systems is planned, reviewed, and approved first. |
| Rounds and readings | You inspect equipment on a schedule and record what you find. |
| Escalation and incident response | You know when to call for help and how to communicate during an event. |

Here is how a maintenance technician might rewrite a vague bullet:

- **Before:** "Did maintenance on building equipment."
- **After:** "Completed scheduled PM on air handlers, pumps, and emergency generators through a CMMS; applied LOTO before every repair; logged daily rounds and escalated abnormal readings to the shift lead."

## Where to look for openings

Data center jobs are posted under many titles, so search broadly. Useful search terms include critical facilities technician, critical environment technician, facilities engineer, data center technician, data center operations technician, NOC technician, and data center security officer.

- **Operators:** the careers pages of colocation providers, cloud and hyperscale companies, and large enterprises that run their own sites.
- **Contractors and commissioning firms:** electrical, mechanical, and controls contractors and commissioning agents working on new builds.
- **Equipment manufacturers and service vendors:** UPS, generator, switchgear, and cooling companies hire field service technicians, and facility services firms staff operating sites.
- **Staffing and recruiting firms** that focus on mission critical facilities.
- **Trade unions and apprenticeship programs**, if you are coming up through a trade.
- **Veteran transition and hiring resources**, if you are leaving the military.
- **Community colleges and local workforce boards**, especially near large data center campuses.

Before you apply, read [what each role does](https://datacentertraining.us/careers/data-center-jobs.html) so you can tailor your resume to the job posting.

## What the first 90 days look like

New hires are usually paired with an experienced technician and work through a site qualification program before they perform critical tasks alone. A typical progression looks like this:

1. **Days 1-30: Learn the site**Safety orientation and the site's LOTO and electrical safety programs. Walk every space with a mentor, learn the one-line diagrams, and find every EPO station and emergency shutoff.
2. **Days 31-60: Work with supervision**Perform rounds, assist with preventive maintenance, escort vendors, and start reading the site's MOPs and EOPs. Learn how each alarm is handled.
3. **Days 61-90: Build independence**Take on assigned maintenance tasks, join drills, complete qualification sign-offs, and ask your lead for honest feedback.

For more detail, read [what a new data center technician should learn in the first 90 days](https://datacentertraining.us/blog/posts/what-a-new-data-center-technician-should-learn-in-the-first-90-days.html).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get a data center job with no experience?

Yes, but it helps to bring something adjacent. Entry-level data center technician, NOC, and security roles often hire people from IT support, retail electronics, or security backgrounds. Facilities roles usually want some electrical, mechanical, or maintenance experience. Learning the basics of power, cooling, and safety first shows an employer you are serious and ready to learn.

### Which certifications do I need to work in a data center?

It depends on the role. Electricians usually need a license from their state or local jurisdiction, and HVAC technicians who open refrigerant circuits need EPA Section 608 certification from an EPA-approved program. IT technicians often add credentials offered by CompTIA or BICSI. Many entry-level roles have no formal requirement, and employers train on site-specific procedures.

### Do data center jobs require a background check?

Many do. Data centers hold equipment and data for banks, healthcare providers, and government agencies, so employers commonly run background checks and some run drug screens. Government facilities may require a security clearance. Be ready to provide your work history and references.

### How long does it take to get hired in a data center?

There is no fixed timeline. People with transferable trade, military, or IT experience can often move quickly once they learn the vocabulary and target the right job titles. People starting from scratch usually need to build hands-on experience first, through a help desk job, an apprenticeship, or facilities maintenance work.
